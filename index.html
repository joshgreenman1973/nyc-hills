Uphill

Four in five miles of New York City street are essentially flat, and the steepest block, on Staten Island’s Chestnut Place, climbs 20 feet for every 100.

This map grades every walkable block in the city, 6,516 miles of street plus paths, boardwalks and step streets, using a federal lidar survey flown in 2013 and 2014. Of those street miles, 5,272 rise less than 2 feet in every 100. Only 36 miles are steeper than 1 foot in 12, the most a wheelchair ramp is allowed under federal accessibility rules, and 19.5 of those miles are on Staten Island. Hover over a block for its grade, or pick two points and the map will find the flattest walk between them.

How walks are chosen

Borough by borough

Staten Island is the hilliest borough: a third of its 970 street miles have some grade to them, and the Bronx is close behind at 29% of 848 miles. Brooklyn, mostly glacial outwash plain south of the ridge that runs through Green-Wood Cemetery and Prospect Park, is the flattest, with 90% of its 1,678 miles under a 2% grade. Each bar shows a borough’s street miles by grade, using the colors on the map.

The steepest streets

The steepest stretch of each named street, among pieces at least 131 feet (40 meters) long, measured end to end. Staten Island’s North Shore hills hold most of the list, with Riverdale in the Bronx and Douglaston in Queens. Stretches within 98 feet (30 meters) of a highway, ramp or bridge are left off, because the ground model can misread them (why). Click a name to go there on the map.

    The highest street in each borough

    The highest point on each borough’s street grid, in feet above sea level (the NAVD 88 datum), from the same survey. Staten Island’s, near the 401-foot summit of Todt Hill, is the highest street in the city; Manhattan’s, beside Bennett Park’s 265-foot rock, is the island’s.

    How this was made

    Everything here can be rebuilt from public data with the scripts in the project’s repository, which also holds a longer methodology. Figures were computed on Nov. 23, 2026.

    Sources

    • Streets: the city’s Street Centerline (CSCL) file on NYC Open Data, downloaded Nov. 23, 2026: 122,331 segments.
    • Elevation: the U.S. Geological Survey’s 3D Elevation Program one-meter bare-earth model, read through the National Map elevation service on a grid of about 2.5 meters. For the whole city it comes from one survey, the USGS New York CMGP Sandy lidar, flown from August 2013 to April 2014. A street regraded since then shows its old slope.
    • The shaded relief under the streets is drawn from Terrain Tiles on AWS, a separate elevation source used only for the picture.

    What counts as walkable

    Kept: streets, alleys, boardwalks, paths and step streets that are built, plus bridges where pedestrians are allowed (these connect the walking network but aren’t graded). Dropped: 9,397 segments of types people can’t walk (highways, ramps, tunnels, driveways, ferry routes and the like), 2,541 marked for vehicles only and 39 not yet built. Segments the city codes as above or below ground level are not graded either. Blocks longer than 100 meters (328 feet) are cut into equal pieces no longer than that, so a hump mid-block shows up. That gives 173,294 pieces, 169,284 of them graded. Street miles count streets and alleys only; where the city’s file draws a divided street as two lines, both count.

    How a grade is measured

    Each line is sampled every 5 meters, and 60 meters past each end along its own direction. The elevation model is bare earth, with bridges removed, so a street that crosses a highway or rail cut on a short bridge appears to plunge into the cut. To undo that, the profile is passed through three filters: a three-sample median that drops single bad readings; a “closing” (a running maximum and then a running minimum over 120 meters) that fills any dip narrower than that; and an “opening” (minimum, then maximum, over 40 meters) that shaves any bump narrower than 40 meters. The ends of 2,323 pieces moved more than 2 meters as a result. Any piece whose filtered profile still rises or falls more than 35% over 10 meters is treated as a wall or bridge abutment and left ungraded: 169 pieces, 4.6 miles.

    Grade is the difference in elevation between a piece’s two ends divided by its length along the line, in percent. The bands: flat, under 2%; gentle, 2% to 5%; noticeable, 5% to 8.33% (federal rules treat a walkway steeper than 1 in 20 as a ramp); steep, 8.33% to 12% (1 in 12 is the steepest ramp allowed); and very steep, 12% or more.

    Stretches near highways and bridges

    The filters don’t catch every case, especially where a street runs alongside a sunken highway or meets a viaduct. Any graded piece within 30 meters of a highway, ramp, bridge, tunnel or off-ground segment is flagged: 471.9 street miles. Flagged pieces stay on the map with a warning in their label and count toward the totals above, but they are kept off the steepest-streets list.

    The steepest streets

    From pieces at least 40 meters long that are named, graded and not flagged, the steepest piece of each street in each borough. As a check, the method puts Duffy’s Hill, the block of Lexington Avenue between 102nd and 103rd streets in East Harlem, at 12.1%; published measurements put it at 12.6%.

    How walks are chosen

    The shortest walk minimizes distance. The flattest walk minimizes distance weighted by steepness: each piece counts as its length times one plus the square of its grade divided by 5%. A 5% block counts double, a 10% block five times and a 2% block barely more than its length. Step streets count 40 times their length, so the flattest walk avoids stairs unless there’s no way around. Bridges, which aren’t graded, count at their length. Each click snaps to the nearest corner or block end within 600 meters. Staten Island has no walkable connection to the other boroughs (the ferry isn’t included), so walks can’t cross between them.

    Limits

    • The survey is from 2013 and 2014, and the streets are from 2026. New streets are graded on the old ground.
    • Grades follow the center of the street. A sidewalk can differ, especially where it steps or ramps.
    • A step street’s grade is its overall rise over its length, not the pitch of the stairs.
    • Very short pieces carry more noise: an error of 10 centimeters is a full point of grade over 10 meters.
